Our story

A store built out of a pushcart habit.

Best of Boston sits in the North Market Building, part of a retail operation that started at Faneuil Hall Marketplace in 1976 and never left.

  1. Home
  2. About

Where it comes from

The marketplace reopened in 1976. So did the business that runs this shop.

Faneuil Hall Marketplace had been a working market since 1826 and a near ruin by the 1960s. It reopened in 1976 as a place to walk through and buy things, and it launched the first retail pushcart programme in the country in the same year.

One of the first specialty stores to open here sold appliquéd canvas bags. It was run by Lucy Mackall, and it grew into a company — Color, Inc. — that spent the next four decades opening city-specific stores in festival marketplaces across the United States. Best of Boston is one of its store concepts, and this is the city it was named for.

The stores are now part of Event Network’s destination retail collection. The address has not moved.

A short chronology

Five dates that explain the shop.

1742

Faneuil Hall opens, with a grasshopper on the roof

The gilded copper grasshopper weathervane goes up over the hall and becomes the symbol of the whole marketplace. We borrowed it for our own mark.

1826

Quincy Market is built behind it

The inscription inside the rotunda still reads that the building has served the people of Boston as the central market since August 1826. Durgin Park opened the same year.

1976

The marketplace reopens and the carts arrive

America’s first retail pushcart programme starts here. So does the specialty-store company that would later run this shop, with a single kiosk selling canvas bags.

1987

The kiosk becomes a store

That first stall turns into Destination Boston in the Quincy Market Building. Other city-specific concepts follow, Best of Boston among them.

Today

Two floors at 354 North Market

Gifts, glassware and small souvenirs downstairs. Apparel and sport upstairs. Open until nine, six days a week, on the Freedom Trail.

How we buy

Everything has to earn its shelf.

A souvenir store lives or dies on edit. The temptation is to stock everything with the city’s name on it; the discipline is to stop. We keep what people actually carry out: the heavy sweatshirt, the mug with thick walls, the magnet that does not look like a magnet.

A good part of the shelf is New England — taffy, maple, felt, canvas. The rest is Boston: the skyline, the cod, the lobster, the red line of the Freedom Trail, and the grasshopper that has been over our heads since 1742.

The visit

What it is like inside.

Two floors A staircase in the middle of the shop. Most people miss it on the way in and find it on the way out.
Packed, on purpose Floor to ceiling. It is a market store, not a showroom, and it is meant to be rummaged through.
Ask at the counter The people behind it know the marketplace by heart, including where to eat and which way the Trail goes.
Open late Until nine Monday to Saturday, seven on Sunday, after most of the city has closed.

Outside the door

The cobblestones do their own programming.

Street performers work the marketplace every day from 11am. You can hear them from inside the shop, which is either a perk or an occupational hazard.
